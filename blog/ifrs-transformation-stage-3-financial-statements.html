IFRS transformation. Stage 3: financial statements

At the diagnostic stage, the statement of financial position, income statement, cash flows, equity and notes give more than just numbers. They show how mature the company's finance function is, and immediately suggest where to look for future IFRS adjustments.

1. Statement of financial position

In the statement of financial position, we are interested in one thing: how realistically assets, liabilities and equity are presented.

It is worth starting with property, plant and equipment - the carrying amount is often overstated or understated, depreciation periods are outdated, component accounting is absent, or revaluation or impairment is needed. Intangible assets deserve a separate look: sometimes assets that are not assets under IFRS sit on the balance sheet, and sometimes the opposite - development costs that could well have been capitalised were written off to expenses.

Inventories should be checked for turnover: slow-moving, obsolete or illiquid balances and the absence of an allowance down to net realisable value are a typical situation. Receivables are a similar story: overdue amounts without an allowance for expected credit losses under IFRS 9 are one of the first things to pay attention to.

Next come cash, loans and borrowings (long-term liabilities that need to be discounted or reclassified) and leases - contracts that under IFRS may give rise to a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. And finally, equity - retained earnings, errors of prior periods, the effect of the transition to IFRS.

Overall, the statement of financial position immediately suggests where to expect the largest transformation adjustments.

2. Income statement

Here the main question is whether the company correctly recognises income and expenses and how exactly it earns money.

Revenue is the first thing we check: whether the timing of recognition is correctly determined under IFRS 15. Cost of sales - whether it is formed correctly, and whether there are capitalised amounts where there should not be any, and vice versa. Gross margin also says a lot: its instability often points to problems with the correct attribution of transactions to the reporting period, with inventories or with the classification of expenses.

Administrative expenses sometimes hide amounts that should be reclassified or capitalised. Finance costs may lack the effects of discounting, leases or amortised cost. Exchange differences raise the question of functional currency and the correctness of foreign currency accounting, and income tax - the need to calculate deferred taxes.

In the end, the income statement shows how "high-quality" the profit is, that is, whether it is recurring and whether it is correctly formed. If a company shows a high profit but has significant overdue receivables, under IFRS part of this profit may simply "dissolve" through the allowance for expected credit losses.

3. Statement of cash flows

Here we check the simplest thing: whether the profit is confirmed by real money.

We look at operating cash flow - whether the business actually generates cash, and at the gap between profit and cash flow, which usually reveals problems with receivables, inventories or prepayments. Investing cash flow shows purchases and sales of property, plant and equipment and capital investments, financing cash flow - loans, borrowings, payments to owners. Separately, we check whether cash balances agree with the statement of financial position, and whether flows are correctly classified between operating, investing and financing activities.

Cash flow, in essence, shows the real liquidity of the business. It happens that a company is profitable according to the income statement, but operating cash flow is negative. This is almost always a signal that cash is stuck in receivables or inventories.

4. Statement of changes in equity

This statement answers the question of what happened to the owners' capital.

We check share capital for consistency with the legal documents, retained earnings - for the accumulated result and errors of prior periods. Dividends - whether payments to owners are correctly presented; owners' contributions - whether they are by any chance hidden in loans or payables. We also look at how the covering of losses was financed, and whether adjustments to prior periods are needed because of the transition to IFRS.

Equity, in essence, shows how the "value" of the business changed for the owners and which transactions may have been classified not entirely correctly. For example, an interest-free loan from an owner under IFRS, depending on its terms, may partly look not simply as a liability, but as an element of the owner's contribution to equity.

5. Notes to the financial statements

The notes are probably the most important block of all. The financial statements show the numbers, and the notes explain why the numbers are what they are.

We start with accounting policies - whether the approaches to revenue recognition and accounting for property, plant and equipment, inventories, provisions and exchange differences are described. Then - disclosures on property, plant and equipment (movements, depreciation, disposals, revaluation), on receivables (overdue periods, allowances, concentration of risk) and on loans (rates, terms, security, covenants (conditions set by the lender)). Separately, we check the disclosure of transactions with related parties - owners, the group, management - as well as court cases, which may conceal potential provisions or contingent liabilities. Equally important are events after the reporting date, management judgements - whether there are significant estimates and assumptions - and risk disclosures.

The notes show how ready the company is for IFRS reporting. And here the main problem often emerges: it is not only about the numbers, but about the company simply having no culture of disclosure. Under IFRS it is not enough to explain what happened, because you have to show which judgements, risks and assumptions stand behind the numbers.
